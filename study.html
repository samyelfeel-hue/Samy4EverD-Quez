🗂️ Topics in this lesson

    🔗 Connection reminder

    🧭 START HERE

    🚀 Picture the kidney as a recycling factory

    Blood enters the kidney, and the nephron decides which salts and water to recycle and which to let leave as urine. Diuretics change that recycling process at different stops.

    💡 Everyday example

    Imagine three checkpoints that normally return salt to the bloodstream. Blocking a checkpoint means more salt continues through the system, taking water along with it.

    1. Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors

    Representative drug: Acetazolamide

    Site: Proximal convoluted tubule

    Mechanism: Carbonic anhydrase inhibition decreases bicarbonate reabsorption, increasing sodium and water excretion.

    Medicinal chemistry: Acetazolamide contains a sulfonamide group.

    ⭐ MUST MEMORIZE

    Acetazolamide → carbonic anhydrase inhibitor → proximal tubule → relatively mild diuretic effect.

    2. Thiazide Diuretics

    🧠 Think of it like this

    At the distal tubule checkpoint, the Na⁺/Cl⁻ transporter is a revolving door. Thiazides stop the door, so less sodium and chloride return to the blood.

    Site: Distal convoluted tubule

    Mechanism: Inhibit the Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter.

    DrugStudy point
    ChlorothiazideThiazide diuretic
    HydrochlorothiazideThiazide diuretic
    IndapamideThiazide-like diuretic

    Adverse effect: Hypokalemia; hypersensitivity is possible with sulfonamide-containing agents.

    ⭐ MUST MEMORIZE

    Thiazides → DCT → Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter → hypokalemia.

    3. Loop Diuretics

    💦 Visual memory

    Picture the loop as the kidney’s powerful salt-recycling station. Furosemide blocks the Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ transporter there, producing a strong diuretic effect.

    Representative drug: Furosemide (Lasix)

    Site: Thick ascending limb of loop of Henle

    Mechanism: Inhibit Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter; produce a strong diuretic effect.

    Lecture emphasis: Relatively rapid onset and short duration.

    ⭐ MUST MEMORIZE

    Furosemide → loop diuretic → strong diuresis.

    📊 AT A GLANCE

    Compare all 3 classes

    ClassWhere?What to remember
    🟣 AcetazolamideProximal tubuleCarbonic anhydrase; mild diuresis
    🩷 ThiazidesDistal tubuleNa⁺/Cl⁻; hypokalemia
    🩵 FurosemideLoop of HenleNa⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻; strong diuresis
    🎯 One-sentence summary

    These diuretics work at different nephron sites, but all reduce salt reabsorption and thereby increase urine production.

    🎭 MEMORY STORY

    Take a journey through the kidney

    Stop 1 — Proximal tubule: Acetazolamide switches off the carbonic anhydrase helper.

    Stop 2 — Loop of Henle: Furosemide closes the big three-ion recycling gate (Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻).

    Stop 3 — Distal tubule: Thiazides close the two-ion gate (Na⁺/Cl⁻).

    🌟 Remember the route

    A → F → T as you travel: Acetazolamide (proximal) → Furosemide (loop) → Thiazides (distal). This is a memory device, not a treatment sequence.

    🎯 TRANSFER YOUR KNOWLEDGE

    Practice linking clues

    A drug acts in the DCT and may cause hypokalemia. Which class and transporter?

    Thiazides. They inhibit the Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter. Connect the location to the mechanism and then to potassium monitoring.

    A drug blocks Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻. What site, class and effect should come to mind?

    Thick ascending loop of Henle → loop diuretic (e.g., furosemide) → strong diuresis.

    How does a medicinal-chemistry clue help with a pharmacology question?

    A structural feature such as a sulfonamide group may help identify a drug family, but structure alone does not establish its exact mechanism. Confirm the specific drug and target.

    What transporter do thiazides inhibit?

    Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule.

    What distinguishes loop diuretics?

    Strong diuretic effect from action in the thick ascending limb.

    Example: A drug blocks the Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter. Which class?

    Thiazide diuretic. The Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter is the key clue.

    Example: Which drug should you associate with a strong loop diuretic effect?

    Furosemide. Remember: loop = powerful salt-recycling checkpoint.

    📚 NEW LESSON • DIURETICS

    4. Potassium-sparing diuretics: keep the K⁺!

    Think of the collecting tubule as the kidney's final checkpoint. Sodium enters cells here, while potassium can be lost into urine. Potassium-sparing drugs interfere with this process.

    🔗 Connect the mechanisms

    Spironolactone blocks the instruction (aldosterone signaling). Amiloride blocks the door (ENaC). Both reduce sodium uptake at the late nephron and tend to spare potassium.

    ⭐ Remember the contrast

    Thiazides/loops: can lower K⁺. Spironolactone/amiloride: can raise K⁺. This is why potassium monitoring matters.

    Lecture connection: CVS slides 14–15.

    📚 NEW LESSON • OSMOTIC DIURETIC

    5. Mannitol: water follows particles

    Mannitol is filtered by the kidney but is not significantly reabsorbed. It increases tubular fluid osmolality, helping retain water in the urine.

    💧 Visual example

    Imagine mannitol particles as passengers staying inside a tube. Water tends to stay with them, rather than being reclaimed into the bloodstream.

    Quick connection

    Other diuretics block enzymes, receptors, or transporters. Mannitol works primarily through an osmotic effect.

    Lecture connection: CVS slide 16.

    🫀 BIG PICTURE

    6. Blood pressure: two levers, one goal

    Think of circulation as water moving through pipes. Blood pressure is influenced by how much blood the heart pumps and how tightly the blood vessels are constricted.

    ⭐ Study connection

    Diuretics mainly influence volume; direct vasodilators mainly influence vascular resistance. Many other cardiovascular drugs act through the nervous system or hormones to influence these factors.

    🧠 NERVOUS SYSTEM CONNECTION

    7. Sympathetic signals: turn down the pressure

    The sympathetic nervous system can increase vascular tone. The lecture groups several antihypertensive drugs by where they interrupt this signaling.

    Drug / groupConnectionMemory cue
    Clonidine, guanfacineCentral α₂ agonists reduce sympathetic outflow🧠 Turn down the brain's signal
    MethyldopaConverted to α-methylnorepinephrine, which activates central α₂ pathways🔁 False transmitter
    Prazosin, terazosin, doxazosinα₁ blockade relaxes blood vessels🚪 Block the vessel's squeeze signal
    GuanethidineReduces norepinephrine stores/release📦 Empty the messenger supply
    🔗 Connect CNS and CVS

    Central α₂ agonists reduce sympathetic output from the brain, affecting the cardiovascular system. That connects central nervous system pharmacology with blood-pressure control—but do not confuse these drugs with CNS stimulants.

    Lecture connection: CVS slides 17–25. Some agents in these slides are historical or infrequently used today.

    🚦 VESSEL MUSCLE CONNECTION

    8. Direct vasodilators: relax the pipes

    Unlike central sympatholytics, direct vasodilators act primarily on vascular smooth muscle.

    DrugKey association
    HydralazineDirect arteriolar vasodilation
    MinoxidilK⁺ channel opening; active minoxidil sulfate metabolite; hypertrichosis
    DiazoxideK⁺ channel opening
    Sodium nitroprussideNO-mediated vasodilation; IV; cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity risk
    🎢 Cause-and-effect example

    When arteries suddenly relax, the body may respond with reflex tachycardia and fluid retention. That explains the lecture's connection between vasodilators and combination treatment with a β-blocker and/or diuretic.

    Lecture connection: CVS slides 26–29.

    🔗 HORMONE CONNECTION

    9. RAAS: the hormone chain that raises pressure

    When the body activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, it promotes vasoconstriction and sodium retention.

    🧩 Connect three drug families

    ACE inhibitors block the enzyme making angiotensin II. ARBs block angiotensin II at the AT₁ receptor. Spironolactone blocks aldosterone signaling farther downstream. Same hormonal pathway, three different checkpoints.

    ClassDrug examplesKey clue
    ACE inhibitorsCaptopril, enalapril, fosinoprilACE zinc-binding chemistry; cough can occur
    ARBsLosartan, irbesartanAT₁ receptor blockade; no bradykinin-mediated cough
    Aldosterone antagonistSpironolactoneMineralocorticoid receptor blockade; K⁺ retention
    ⭐ Medicinal chemistry connection

    Captopril has a sulfhydryl group. Enalapril is an ester prodrug converted to enalaprilat. Fosinopril uses a phosphinate zinc-binding group. Losartan and irbesartan are ARBs, not ACE inhibitors.

    Lecture connection: CVS slides 30–34. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated during pregnancy; these notes are for exam study, not personal treatment decisions.

    🏆 CHAPTER CONNECTION SUMMARY

    One condition, several routes to lower pressure

    Volume route: Diuretics increase salt/water excretion.
    Brain route: Central α₂ agonists reduce sympathetic outflow.
    Vessel route: α₁ blockers and vasodilators reduce vascular tone.
    Hormone route: ACE inhibitors, ARBs and aldosterone antagonists interrupt RAAS.

    🧠 Remember: V–B–V–H

    Volume → Brain → Vessels → Hormones. These are different entry points into the same blood-pressure story.

    Why can spironolactone and an ARB both affect potassium?

    Both reduce aldosterone effects—spironolactone at the mineralocorticoid receptor, and an ARB upstream by blocking angiotensin II signaling. Both can contribute to hyperkalemia.

    Why can ACE inhibitors cause cough while ARBs usually do not?

    ACE also breaks down bradykinin. Inhibiting ACE can increase bradykinin; ARBs block AT₁ without directly inhibiting bradykinin breakdown.

    What connects clonidine and prazosin?

    Both reduce sympathetic effects on blood pressure, but clonidine acts centrally at α₂ receptors, while prazosin blocks peripheral α₁ receptors.
